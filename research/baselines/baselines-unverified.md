## Angle 1: Throughput reference points for fast RL simulators, to contextualise Furrow's §2.5 targets (≥2M primitive steps/s and ≥20k macro steps/s aggregate on a 16-core 

**Summary.** Throughput reference points for fast RL simulators, to contextualise Furrow's §2.5 targets (≥2M primitive steps/s and ≥20k macro steps/s aggregate on a 16-core desktop, reset ≤50 µs). Headline numbers in the literature span six orders of magnitude and are NOT comparable without their measurement conventions: EnvPool's "1M Atari FPS" on a 256-core DGX-A100 is raw frames (×4 frameskip) with random actions, i.e. ~267k agent steps/s; Sample Factory's "pure simulation" Atari is 181,740 frames/s on 36 cores (~1.3k agent steps/s/core); NLE runs 14.4k steps/s single-process on a 2020 MacBook (39k/core on an i9-14900K per PufferLib); MiniHack ~11k/core; Procgen 13–25k/core; Crafter (Python) ~320/core with 80% of time in reset and only 1,580 steps/s end-to-end with 1,024 SB3 workers; Craftax (JAX) 266k–405k end-to-end training steps/s on one RTX 4090; PufferLib Ocean C envs >1M steps/s per core (some 10M) with 300k–1.2M end-to-end training on a 4090 and 3–5M in 3.0; Minecraft (MineRL/MineDojo/Malmö) 57–133 steps/s; GPU physics (Isaac Gym 540–700k, MJX 0.95–3.35M humanoid, Brax "millions" to "hundreds of millions" on TPU pods; Madrona 2–40M on a 4090). The only Rust RL envs with published numbers are toy classic-control crates (Operant 97.5M SIMD CartPole steps/s, self-reported, no hardware given; rlox 2.7M steps/s at 512 envs on an M3 Pro), so a Rust farming-sim at 125k primitive steps/s/core would be a new reference point between NetHack-class (39k/core) and Ocean-class (>1M/core) CPU environments. Best-practice measurement consensus: report agent steps (state ticks separately), random AND trained/expert policy, ≥60 s or ≥50k-iteration runs with warmup excluded, single-core SPS plus vectorized sweeps (num_envs ≈ cores, then 2–3× oversubscription), % time in reset and step-time variance, end-to-end training SPS as % of pure simulation, full hardware (CPU model, P/E cores, NUMA, threads, build flags), and an explicit statement of observation copy semantics (preallocated shared buffers, one copy to NumPy).

### 1.1 EnvPool (C++ thread-pool vector env) peaks at 1,069,922 Atari FPS and 3,134,287 MuJoCo FPS on a 256-core DGX-A100 (AMD EPYC 7742, 8 NUMA nodes) in numa+async mode; on a 12-core laptop (i7-8750H) it reaches 49,439 Atari / 105,126 MuJoCo FPS (async) vs 15,863 / 36,586 for Python subprocess vector env and 4,893 / 12,861 for a for-loop; on a 32-thread Ryzen 9 5950X workstation 200,428 / 582,446 FPS (async).

Table 1 of the paper (ar5iv) and the README/benchmark page agree. The README adds a 96-core TPU-VM tier (EnvPool numa+async 373,169 Atari / 896,830 MuJoCo). Headline speedups: '14.9x / 19.6x of the gym.vector_env baseline' at 256 cores and '3.1x / 2.9x' on 12 cores. Single-env Pong on the laptop: Python 4,891 → EnvPool 7,887 FPS. Benchmark settings: sync `--num-envs 12 --batch-size 12`, async `--num-envs 36 --batch-size 12`, numa+async `--num-envs 100 --batch-size 32 --thread-affinity-offset -1` run per NUMA node via numactl.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Shows what a C/C++ engine achieves per core for ALE/MuJoCo-class workloads: ~4k Atari FPS/core and ~12k MuJoCo FPS/core even at 256 cores, with sub-linear scaling (for-loop 4,640 on DGX vs 7,914 on workstation due to lower clocks). Furrow's 16-core target of 2M primitive steps/s (125k/core) is far above ALE/MuJoCo per-core rates and must come from a lighter per-step workload, not parallelism alone.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10558, https://github.com/sail-sg/envpool, https://envpool.readthedocs.io/en/latest/content/benchmark.html

### 1.2 EnvPool's headline numbers are RAW FRAMES with random actions, not agent decisions: the benchmark page states 'FPS is multiplied with frame_skip (4 for Atari and 5 for Mujoco)', runs are sized so 'each test run for about one minute', and the paper measures 'a mean of 50K iterations' with randomly sampled actions. So 1.07M Atari FPS ≈ 267k agent steps/s and 3.13M MuJoCo FPS ≈ 627k agent steps/s.

Paper guidance also includes: set num_envs to 2–3x the thread count for async mode, pin threads to cores, and run one EnvPool instance per NUMA node to cut contention on the action queue. Design note: 'the pointer to the target slot of memory is directly passed to the environment execution' and blocks are 'converted into NumPy arrays via pybind11 when the block of memory is marked as ready', removing the inter-process copy and the batching copy present in Python subprocess vector envs. End-to-end: CleanRL PPO Breakout wall-clock went from 200 min (for-loop) to ~73 min (EnvPool sync); Pong in ~5 min on a laptop.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow has an analogous ambiguity (1 primitive step = 1 tick, but Wait = 10 ticks and macros = hundreds of ticks). The paper must state whether it reports agent steps or ticks; reporting both avoids the 4–5x inflation seen in frame-based headlines.
- sources: https://envpool.readthedocs.io/en/latest/content/benchmark.html, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10558

### 1.3 Sample Factory (ICML 2020) reports 'pure simulation' upper bounds on a 36-core/72-thread dual Xeon Gold 6154 + RTX 2080Ti server of 181,740 env frames/s (Atari Breakout, 84x84 grayscale), 322,907 (VizDoom 128x72) and 49,679 (DMLab 96x72); its APPO trainer reaches 135,893 / 146,551 / 42,149 FPS = 74.8% / 45.4% / 84.8% of pure simulation, vs SeedRL 39,726 and rlpyt 68,880 on Atari. FPS counts frames with 4-frameskip ('4x the number of samples processed by the learner').

System #1 (i9-7900X 10c/20t, GTX 1080Ti) peaks at 48,016 Atari FPS with 640 envs. Architecture: double-buffered sampling (two env groups alternate between CPU stepping and GPU policy inference), trajectory data in shared tensors with only indices passed through FIFO queues, stateless GPU policy workers. Appendix recommends one rollout worker per logical core.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Establishes (a) a per-core ALE reference of ~5k frames/s ≈ 1.3k agent steps/s/core on server CPUs, and (b) the reporting convention of 'end-to-end training throughput as % of pure simulation', which Furrow's RESULTS.md should adopt for its PPO/DAgger loops.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.11751, https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11751

### 1.4 PufferLib 2.0 (RLJ/RLC 2025) reports single-core steps/s (with its emulation layer) on a 24-core i9-14900K desktop: NetHack 39k (64% overhead from emulation of its structured obs), MiniHack 11k, Procgen Bigfish 25k, Minigrid 16k, CartPole 270k, Ocean Squared 240k, Neural MMO 2 2,400 (68% of time in reset), Atari Breakout 1.2k (54% in reset), Pokemon Red 700, Crafter 320 (80% of time in reset). Puffer Ocean C environments 'each simulate at >1M steps/second on a single CPU core', 'Some environments run 10M steps/second'.

Table 1 columns are SPS, % Reset, % Step STD, % Overhead — a useful template. Table 2 vectorized throughput on the desktop (D) and a 6-core i7-10750H laptop (L): NetHack PufferLib 96k/18k vs Gymnasium 7k/7k vs SB3 6k; CartPole 460k/110k (PufferLib) and 3M/200k (Puffer Pool) vs Gymnasium 82k/14k and SB3 100k/11k; Crafter 360/170, 2.8k/450 with pool, Gymnasium 340/135, SB3 350/203; Atari Breakout 11.8k/2k, 25.6k/3k pool, Gymnasium 4.8k/2.5k; Procgen Bigfish 33k/6.2k, 150k/9.6k pool. Training: PPO demo 'trains Ocean environments at 300k-1.2M steps/second on a single RTX 4090' with 150k–1M-parameter MLP/CNN-LSTM models; Atari training up to 30k SPS. 3.0 note added during review: Ocean >25 envs, 'small models now train 3-5M steps/second', Ocean Pong solved in 3–5 s and Breakout in 20–30 s on one RTX 5090; Neural MMO 3 trained for 640B steps in 3 d 8 h on 6 RTX 4090s. Caveat in paper: these benchmarks 'are run without a model in a loop' (environment-only), and the desktop chip has 8 performance + 16 efficiency cores, which hurts synchronous vectorizers that wait on the slowest core.

- confidence: high
- relevance: This is the most directly comparable table for a Furrow paper: a Rust grid farming sim at 125k primitive steps/s per core would sit between NetHack-class roguelikes (39k/core, C) and Ocean arcade envs (>1M/core, C). Also shows that reset/day-rollover cost can dominate (Crafter 80%), which motivates Furrow's explicit reset ≤50 µs target and separate per-day-rollover timing.
- sources: https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf

### 1.5 PufferLib's vectorization design (the reason it beats Gymnasium/SB3 multiprocessing by 5–30x at high step rates): observations/rewards/terminals/actions live in large shared-memory arrays that C environments write directly ('simulate their observations directly into shared memory'); workers busy-wait on an unlocked shared flag instead of using queues; pipes are used only for non-empty infos (once per episode); multiple envs per worker stack into preallocated arrays with no extra copies; a zero-copy batching path waits on a contiguous subset of workers. It notes 'Gymnasium and SB3 multiprocessing implementations experience significant scaling degradation above 1000 synchronizations per second per core' and 'main process overhead has to be optimized to within a few microseconds'.

The paper describes four separately optimised code paths (sync: no extra copy; async: one copy; single-worker batch: no copy; zero-copy circular buffer) and an autotune utility that benchmarks valid vectorization settings. Also notable: the emulation layer 'adds a few tens of microseconds' and was 'Cythonized and tested to be faster than a half dozen implementations... including efforts to write it in C and Rust' — a reminder that FFI boundary design, not language, decides overhead. puffer.ai docs (current) describe PuffeRL as 'Up to 60,000,000 step/second training in only ~10k lines of CUDA C' with env instances chunked into buffers on separate CUDA streams, OMP threading within a buffer, pinned memory, and tensors 'allocated' once with 'No tensors ... created or reallocated afterwards'; environment guidance: 'Zero them at the start of puf_step to be safe. One memset is usually the quickest.'

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow's VecEnv (rayon threads in one process, preallocated buffers, one copy to NumPy after GIL re-acquire) already avoids the process-sync problem; the remaining cost centres to measure are the Python call overhead per VecEnv.step (must be microseconds) and the obs copy-out. The pool/async idea (return the first N<<M finished envs) is relevant for macro mode, where step durations vary by orders of magnitude (Wait30 vs MineDescend).
- sources: https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf, https://puffer.ai/docs.html

### 1.6 Craftax (ICML 2024, JAX) measured END-TO-END training steps/s on one RTX 4090 + i9-13900K (24c/32t, 32 GB): Craftax-Classic 405,618 (4,096 envs), Craftax 266,961 (4,096), Procgen 7,638 (1,024 SB3 workers), NetHack 5,628 (64 workers, IMPALA baseline), Crafter 1,580 (1,024 SB3 SubprocVecEnv workers), MineRL 133 (8 threads, random actions, 'likely overestimates'). Craftax-Classic is 257x and Craftax 169x faster than Crafter; 1B steps of PPO 'finishes in under an hour using only a single GPU' (51 minutes).

Workers were doubled 'until the machine crashed from an out-of-memory error'. Symbolic observations run 'around 10x faster' than pixel observations; 'optimistic resets' give ~2x. The authors caution 'There is no way to perfectly compare all these environments' because network sizes/epochs/minibatches were only approximately matched. Crafter README confirms observations are 64x64x3 images with 17 actions and a 1M-step interaction budget, with no official speed claim.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Craftax is the closest published analogue to Furrow (Crafter-like survival/crafting with NetHack-style depth) and the obvious comparison row. Its method — 'We measure the average steps per second over the entire RL learning process, unlike other works which report times from running the environment with a static/random agent' — is the convention Furrow's RESULTS.md should include alongside env-only numbers.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16801v2

### 1.8 Minecraft-based environments run at tens to low hundreds of steps/s and lack native vectorization: Craftium (2024) measured MineDojo at 71.87 ± 11.82 steps/s vs Craftium 2746.69 ± 230.41 and VizDoom 2091.91 ± 59.03 (random actions, 64x64 RGB for MineDojo/Craftium, 320x240 VizDoom, 5 runs x 1K steps x 3 envs, NVIDIA A5000 + Intel Xeon Silver 4309Y), noting 'MineDojo lacks parallel environment support' and that Xvfb headless rendering 'causes significant performance drawbacks'; Craftium exceeds 12K steps/s with 16 parallel envs on 16 cores.

Independent community benchmark (yhs0602/minecraft-simulator-benchmark) on a Threadripper 3960X 24-core + RTX 3090 Ti headless (vglrun): Malmö/MineRL 57.7 ± 0.7 FPS at 64x64 render, 55.9 at 640x360; with SB3 PPO in the loop 44.7 and 30.0 FPS; CraftGround (C++ reimplementation) 160.7 FPS (2.79x). Craftax's Table 1 gives MineRL 133 steps/s with 8 threads of random actions; NLE's 2020 table gives MineRLNavigateDense-v0 0.06K SPS single-process on a MacBook.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Directly contextualises Furrow's SDV bridge tier (design doc estimates 10^2–10^3 ticks/s lockstep in the real game): driving a real commercial game yields ~10^2 steps/s, i.e. 3–4 orders of magnitude below a purpose-built sim, which is the quantitative justification for the two-environment architecture.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03969v2, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yhs0602/minecraft-simulator-benchmark/main/README.md, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16801v2, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13760

### 1.9 Gymnasium's built-in vector envs are the slow baseline everyone beats: PufferLib measured Gymnasium AsyncVectorEnv at 82k CartPole steps/s and 7k NetHack steps/s on a 24-core desktop (vs 3M and 96k for PufferLib), and attributes it to per-step process synchronisation and 'multiple small copy operations and additional Python logic' in its structured shared-memory path. Gymnasium docs: AsyncVectorEnv `shared_memory=True` 'can improve the efficiency if the observations are large (e.g. images)', `copy=True` returns copies of observations, `max_concurrency` limits simultaneous reset/step when envs exceed cores.

Gymnasium's docs state only that vector envs 'can provide a linear speed-up' and give no absolute numbers. Info format since v0.25 is a dict of per-key NumPy arrays.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow exposes a gymnasium.vector.VectorEnv wrapper over the native VecEnv (§9); the benchmark should show that wrapper adds negligible overhead versus the raw PyO3 VecEnv, and should never route through AsyncVectorEnv subprocesses.
- sources: https://gymnasium.farama.org/api/vector/async_vector_env/, https://gymnasium.farama.org/api/vector/, https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf

### 1.10 Gymnasium defines three autoreset modes (farama.org/Vector-Autoreset-Mode): NEXT_STEP (default; a finished sub-env is reset on the following step call, so training code must 'beware of episode boundaries'), SAME_STEP ('reset in the same step call', returned obs is the new episode's first obs and 'the final observation of the episode stored in info["final_obs"]'; 'every step produces a valid transition' but 'some vector wrappers do not support this mode'), and DISABLED (manual masked reset, 'can be slower in some cases').

ALE vector env guide (native C++, multithreaded) adds tuning guidance: 'set num_envs close to the number of physical CPU cores', 'A smaller batch size can improve latency while a larger batch size can improve throughput', `thread_affinity_offset` for core pinning, and a split `send`/`recv` API 'for better overlapping of computation and environment stepping'. It publishes no throughput numbers.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow's §3.2 semantics (auto-reset in the same call, last global obs in `final_global`) are exactly SAME_STEP; the Gymnasium wrapper should declare metadata['autoreset_mode'] = AutoresetMode.SAME_STEP and map final_global to info['final_obs'] so Gymnasium-aware trainers compute bootstrapping correctly. ALE's C++ vector env also offers both same-step and next-step modes.
- sources: https://farama.org/Vector-Autoreset-Mode, https://ale.farama.org/vector-environment/

### 1.11 Brax (NeurIPS 2021 D&B) reports 'millions of simulation steps per second' for MuJoCo-Ant-like environments on a single accelerator and 'hundreds of millions of steps per second' on a TPUv3 8x8 pod, versus '~thousands of steps per second' for OpenAI Gym MuJoCo Ant single-threaded; PPO uses num_envs 2048 and reaches performant Ant locomotion 'in ten seconds or so' versus 'close to half an hour' for standard PPO.

Confidence medium because the paper gives exact numbers only in figures (Fig. 2 'effective environment steps per second ... on a 4x2 TPU v3'); text quantities are order-of-magnitude. CPU baselines in appendix: 128-core Xeon @2.2 GHz for MuJoCo, 32-core Xeon for the 32x-CPU curve.

- confidence: medium
- relevance: Marks the GPU/TPU-batched regime Furrow is NOT in (CPU, branching game logic). Useful for the paper's related-work framing: JAX/GPU sims win by 100–1000x only when thousands of envs are batched and control flow is data-parallel; a Rust CPU sim competes on per-core efficiency and latency instead.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.13281

### 1.12 MuJoCo's MJX documentation (2025/26) gives single-humanoid throughput of 650K, 1.8M, 950K and 2.7M steps/s for, respectively, CPU MuJoCo on an Apple M3 Max, CPU MuJoCo on a 64-core AMD 3995WX (testspeed with 2x numcore threads), MJX-JAX on an A100 (batch 8192), and MJX-JAX on an 8-chip TPU v5 (batch 16384); it warns 'for a single scene, MJX-JAX can be 10x slower than MuJoCo' and 'works best when simulating thousands or tens of thousands of scenes in parallel'. MJX-Warp humanoid: 3.35M SPS pure Warp, 2.96M via JAX FFI, dropping to 0.80M if a CUDA graph recapture is forced every step.

Confidence medium on the hardware-to-number mapping: the page lists the four values 'for the four timed architectures ... respectively' and the mapping to M3 Max / 3995WX / A100 / TPU v5 follows the order of the surrounding text. The page recommends `mjx-testspeed --mjcf=... --base_path=.` which reports total simulation time, total steps/s and realtime factor.

- confidence: medium
- relevance: Shows (a) a well-optimised CPU engine still does ~28k steps/s per core on a 64-core workstation, and (b) GPU numbers are highly sensitive to compile/capture effects — the analogue for Furrow's benchmark is to exclude first-call effects (page faults, content loading, cold caches) from timed regions and to report per-core as well as aggregate numbers.
- sources: https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html

### 1.13 Isaac Gym (2021) reports GPU-resident simulation of up to 700K Ant environment steps/s (A100, sweep to 8,192 envs), an average of 540K steps/s during training with 4,096 envs, 200K for Humanoid, and 150K for Shadow Hand with 16,384 envs; Ant reaches 3000 reward in 20 s and Humanoid 5000 in under 4 min on one A100; AMP humanoid training went from ~30 h on 16 CPU cores (PyBullet) to ~6 min ('300x').

The OpenAI Shadow Hand comparison: '384 16-core CPUs with 6144 CPU cores in total' plus 8 V100s for 30 h, versus under 1 h on one A100.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Another GPU reference point; also demonstrates reporting 'average simulation performance achieved during training' separately from peak sweep numbers (700K vs 540K) — Furrow should likewise report peak env-only and in-training averages.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10470

### 1.14 Madrona (SIGGRAPH 2023 engine behind GPUDrive/PufferDrive-style sims) lists RTX 4090 throughput of 2M steps/s for a Hide-and-Seek port, 40M steps/s for Overcooked-AI and 20M steps/s for Hanabi, '1.9 million environment steps per second on a single GPU' for the 3D hide-and-seek paper benchmark, and claims 'two to three orders of magnitude over open source CPU baselines and 5x–33x over strong baselines running on a 32-thread CPU'. PufferDrive 2.0 (PufferLib-based driving sim) trains end-to-end at ~300–320K steps/s on an RTX 4080 vs ~30–50K for GPUDrive and ~2K for Nocturne.

World counts for the Overcooked/Hanabi figures are not given on the page; the hide-and-seek replay demo runs 32,000 concurrent worlds. PufferDrive page figures differ slightly between abstract (300K) and table (320K), and GPUDrive between text (~30K) and table (50K).

- confidence: medium
- relevance: Shows the 'strong 32-thread CPU baseline' gap to GPU is only 5–33x for well-written CPU sims — i.e. a tight Rust CPU sim at ~2M steps/s is within an order of magnitude of GPU ECS engines for grid-world-scale logic, which is a defensible positioning statement for the Furrow paper.
- sources: https://madrona-engine.github.io/, https://emerge-lab.github.io/PufferDrive/pufferdrive-2.0.html

### 1.15 Rust-based RL environments with published throughput are so far limited to classic-control toys: Operant (Rust + PyO3, AVX2 f32x8 SIMD, struct-of-arrays, 'zero-copy numpy') self-reports 97.54M CartPole steps/s at 4,096 envs vs 0.16M for Gymnasium ('~600x'), with no hardware, duration or warmup stated and a Rust nightly requirement; rlox 1.2.0 (Rust data plane via PyO3 + Rayon, 'zero-copy where possible') reports 'Parallel env stepping (2.7M steps/s at 512 envs)' and an end-to-end 256x2048 rollout in 669 ms vs 2,057 ms for SB3 (3.1x) on an Apple M3 Pro 12-core, CPU only (measured 2026-04-08 vs SB3 2.7.1).

Operant's numbers are self-reported with no independent verification and the GitHub page lists no published packages despite PyPI/crates.io listings; treat as a toy upper bound (4 floats of state, SIMD across envs) rather than a game-sim reference. Other Rust crates found (gmgn, border, fgbase/fastgym) publish no throughput.

- confidence: medium
- relevance: There is no established Rust game-sim RL benchmark with published SPS, so Furrow would be a novel reference point; it also means Furrow's comparison table should use C/C++/JAX envs (Ocean, NLE, Procgen, Craftax) rather than Rust peers, and should avoid Operant-style unverifiable claims by publishing hardware, method and scripts.
- sources: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/galenoshea/operant/main/README.md, https://pypi.org/project/rlox/

### 1.16 Measurement conventions that the strongest sources share: (1) random-policy env-only runs of fixed duration — NLE 60 s single process; EnvPool 'mean of 50K iterations' with total steps scaled so each run lasts ~1 min; Craftium 5 runs x 1K steps x 3 tasks with mean ± std; (2) sweep num_envs from 1 to the core count and beyond (EnvPool sweeps 1–12/32/96/256 envs; Sample Factory 20–1,728 envs; Craftax doubled workers until OOM); (3) report decomposition — PufferLib's % Reset, % Step STD and % Overhead columns; Sample Factory's % of pure simulation; (4) report end-to-end training SPS with the real policy (Craftax, Isaac Gym 'average during training').

Additional hardware-reporting details seen across sources: CPU model and physical/logical cores (Sample Factory Table A.1), NUMA node count and `numactl` usage (EnvPool), P-core/E-core heterogeneity affecting synchronous vectorizers (PufferLib: 8P+16E i9-14900K), GPU model for end-to-end numbers, and package versions/dates (rlox, NLE 'latest available packages as of May 2020').

- confidence: high
- relevance: Defines the protocol Furrow's `furrow bench` CLI and the throughput section of papers/furrow should implement so its numbers are directly comparable to the literature.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13760, https://envpool.readthedocs.io/en/latest/content/benchmark.html, https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03969v2, https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.11751, https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16801v2, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.10470

### 1.17 Observation-copy handling is a first-order throughput factor at >100k steps/s and the fast engines all converge on the same pattern: environments write directly into preallocated, contiguous batch buffers (EnvPool passes 'the pointer to the target slot of memory' to the env thread and exposes finished blocks as NumPy arrays via pybind11; PufferLib envs 'simulate their observations directly into shared memory' and only its fully-async path needs one extra copy; Isaac Gym/MJX/PufferLib-CUDA keep everything device-resident with pinned memory). PufferLib notes a naive design 'would require two or three copies' and that Gymnasium's structured shared memory path needs 'multiple small copy operations'.

At 2M steps/s with ~1–2 KB of observation per step, obs bandwidth is 2–4 GB/s — well within memory bandwidth but large enough that an extra copy or Python-side concatenation would be visible in profiles.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow's VecEnv contract (preallocated Rust buffers, one copy into fresh NumPy arrays after the GIL is reacquired) is one copy per step; the benchmark should quantify this copy (grid u8 C x 13 x 13 plus global f32 vector per env) by timing step() with and without write_obs and by comparing a zero-copy variant (e.g., writing into caller-provided NumPy buffers) so the paper can state the overhead rather than assume it is negligible.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10558, https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf, https://puffer.ai/docs.html

### 1.18 Per-core CPU throughput reference ladder (single env, random or near-random policy, consumer/workstation CPUs, from the sources above): Minecraft (MineRL/MineDojo/Malmö) 60–130; Crafter (Python) ~320; ALE Atari ~1–4k agent steps/s (0.9K Montezuma no-frameskip on a 2020 MacBook; ~1.3k/core implied by Sample Factory pure simulation; EnvPool single Pong 7,887 frames/s ÷ 4 ≈ 2k); MiniHack ~11k; Procgen 13–25k; NetHack 14–39k; MuJoCo humanoid ~28k/core (1.8M on 64 cores) to Ant ~12k frames/s/core; Minigrid 16k (Python); CartPole (Python) 77–270k; PufferLib Ocean C arcade envs >1M (some 10M).

Confidence medium because the ladder mixes hardware generations (2020 MacBook i7 vs 2023–2025 i9/Threadripper) and policies; it is meant as order-of-magnitude placement, consistent with NLE's remark that 'the difference in terms of magnitude' is what is stable across machines.

- confidence: medium
- relevance: Furrow's §2.5 target of ≥2M primitive steps/s on 16 cores is ~125k steps/s per core: 3x NetHack-class, 10x Procgen-class, and 8x below Ocean-class arcade envs. That is plausible for a tile-grid sim whose primitive step is mostly a move/turn with no allocation, but macro steps (A* pathing, day rollover with crop/forage/mine regeneration) will cost orders of magnitude more per call — hence the separate ≥20k macro steps/s target (~1.25k/core, NeuralMMO-class).
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13760, https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_151.pdf, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.11751, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.10558, https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/mjx.html, https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03969v2

**Recommendations.**

- Report Furrow throughput in AGENT STEPS per second as the primary metric and game TICKS per second as a secondary one (state the ticks/step ratio for macro mode), mirroring how EnvPool/Sample Factory disclose frame-skip; never present tick counts as steps.
- Implement a `furrow bench` CLI that reproduces three published protocols and emits JSON for papers/furrow: (a) NLE-style single-env, single-thread, uniformly random policy for 60 s; (b) EnvPool-style VecEnv sweep over num_envs in {1, 2, 4, ..., cores, 2x cores, 3x cores} and num_threads in {1, cores/2, cores}, each run sized to ~60 s or >=50k iterations; (c) Craftax-style end-to-end training SPS averaged over a full BC/DAgger/PPO run, reported as a percentage of (b)'s pure-simulation peak (Sample Factory convention).
- Benchmark with BOTH a random policy and the Rust expert (and ideally a trained policy), because NLE explicitly warns that throughput is state-dependent; random agents in Furrow will never reach the mines, fishing or shops, so random-policy SPS will overstate real throughput. Report per-policy numbers side by side.
- Decompose time the way PufferLib does: % time in reset, % in day-rollover (06:00 world update: crop growth, forage spawn, mine regeneration, shipping), step-time standard deviation, and Python/PyO3 overhead (time step() from Python vs the same loop in Rust). - Always publish a hardware line with: CPU model, physical vs logical cores and P/E-core split (PufferLib shows hybrid i9 chips distort synchronous vectorizers), NUMA nodes, RAM, Rust toolchain version, build flags (release, LTO, target-cpu), thread count and pinning, OS; for end-to-end numbers add GPU model and torch version. Treat the design doc's 'target 16-core desktop' as a named configuration in the results table.

## Angle 2: Evaluation-methodology research for the Furrow/SDV speedrun papers, based on ~30 primary sources read in full (arXiv PDFs, official docs). Three consistent mess

**Summary.** Evaluation-methodology research for the Furrow/SDV speedrun papers, based on ~30 primary sources read in full (arXiv PDFs, official docs). Three consistent messages emerge. (1) Statistics: the field's 3-5 seed habit is indefensible — Henderson et al. got p=0.0016 between two 5-seed groups of the same algorithm; Colas et al. show N≈20 seeds are needed for 0.8 power at a 1-SD effect and that the bootstrap test must not be used below N=20-50; Agarwal et al. (rliable) show median scores need 50-100 runs on Atari-100k while IQM + stratified-bootstrap percentile CIs are reliable from N≈10 runs; Gorsane et al.'s standard protocol fixes R=10 training runs, 32 eval episodes per interval, 320 for final/absolute performance, and an (R × tasks) matrix fed to rliable. Patterson et al. add: never report standard errors, use paired tests because seeds are shared, Bonferroni-correct multiple baselines, plot versus environment steps. (2) Benchmarks: Procgen and NLE both evaluate on disjoint held-out seeds (NLE: 1000 unseen-seed episodes; Procgen: 200/500 training levels vs the full distribution, up to 10,000 levels to close the gap); Crafter aggregates 22 achievements by a geometric mean of success rates computed per seed within a fixed 1M-step budget (humans 50.5%); the NetHack Challenge ranked by (ascensions, median, mean) over 4,096 episodes and documents metric gaming; MineRL averaged over 100 episodes under an 8M-sample / 144h budget; BASALT used pairwise human judgements -> TrueSkill; VPT and DreamerV3 report time-boxed success fractions (2.5% diamond pickaxe in 10 min; 10 seeds 'to reliably report the fraction of runs that achieve diamonds') and VPT explicitly notes the median time-to-diamond is unidentifiable (>20 min) because <50% of episodes finish — the censoring problem our days-to-complete metric has. (3) Tools that solve our specific metric: Kaplan-Meier completion curves + log-rank test + restricted mean (RMST) difference handle episodes truncated at max_days without bias; SPL (Anderson et al.) gives a success-weighted efficiency score that maps directly to 'success weighted by inverse days'; Dolan-Moré performance profiles (the basis of rliable's profiles) were designed for 'time-to-solve with failures'; Decision-Transformer-style target-vs-achieved plots and METR-style logistic success-vs-budget fits evaluate the time-conditioned policy; NeurIPS checklist / Green AI / Dodge et al. define what compute and tuning-budget information to report. The recommendations section turns this into a concrete protocol (metrics, plots, tables, tests) for 'days to complete the restoration objective' with failures.

### 2.1 Agarwal et al. (NeurIPS 2021, 'Statistical Precipice'/rliable) document that 3-10 runs per task are the norm in deep RL and show point estimates from such runs are unreliable: on Atari 100k, median scores need 50-100 runs for tight 95% CIs, and 'statistically defensible improvements with median scores is only achieved for 25 runs (ℓ=25) and 100 runs (ℓ=10)'; with a 0% true lift 'even 100 runs are insufficient, with deviations of 20% possible'.

Prior Atari-100k results were 'computed mostly from 3 or 5 runs, and more rarely, 10 or 20 runs'. '5 runs each on 50+ Atari 2600 games in ALE using standard protocol requires more than 1000 GPU training days.' The paper's Table 1 recommendations: (a) interval estimates via stratified bootstrap CIs instead of point estimates; (b) performance profiles (score distributions: fraction of runs above normalized score τ, pooled over runs×tasks) instead of per-task mean tables; (c) IQM ('discards the bottom and top 25% of the runs and calculates the mean score of the remaining 50% runs', i.e. over the N·M run×task scores) instead of mean/median; plus optimality gap ('amount by which the algorithm fails to meet a minimum score of γ=1.0') and average probability of improvement P(X>Y)=1/M Σ_m P(X_m>Y_m) (Mann-Whitney-U based; 'does not account for the size of improvement'). Validation: 'percentile CIs provide good interval estimates for as few as N=10 runs for both median and IQM scores', but 'with 3 runs, bootstrap CIs underestimate the true 95% CIs and might require a larger nominal coverage rate'. They 'recommend using confidence intervals ... and showing effect sizes' and 'avoid statistical significance tests ... because of their dichotomous nature'; 'when CIs overlap, properly accounting for uncertainty entails computing CIs for score differences'. The rliable README expects a score matrix of shape (num_runs × num_games) per algorithm and uses reps=50000 bootstrap replications for aggregate CIs and reps=2000 for probability of improvement.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Defines the core reporting stack for both papers: per-policy score matrix (training runs × evaluation tasks), IQM + optimality gap + probability of improvement with 95% stratified bootstrap CIs, and performance profiles. Sets a minimum of ~10 training seeds per method for the CI machinery to be trustworthy; Furrow's 10^5-10^6 steps/s makes far more affordable.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google-research/rliable/master/README.md

### 2.2 Colas, Sigaud & Oudeyer ('Hitchhiker's Guide', 2019) empirically compared tests and conclude: use Welch's t-test with a lowered α (<0.05, e.g. 0.01); never use the bootstrap test below N=50 seeds nor the permutation test below N=10; Mann-Whitney and the ranked t-test inflate false positives when distributions differ in shape; for 0.8 power you need N≈100 seeds for a relative effect ε=0.5, N≈20 for ε=1, N≈5-10 for ε=2, and 'sample sizes like N=5 would require 3 to 4 times larger effects'.

Real-data case: SAC vs TD3 on Half-Cheetah (192 runs each) gives effect ε=0.93 (means) / 0.80 (medians); detecting it at power 0.8 needs N=10-15 seeds (means) or 15-20 (medians). Non-normal distributions keep the empirical false-positive rate above α for Welch's test (≈0.1 when one distribution is skewed), hence the advice to set α=0.01. Multiple comparisons: the family-wise error rate grows with the number of comparisons; apply Bonferroni α/n_c (at the cost of power). They also recommend comparing full learning curves (per evaluation step) and stating the performance measure clearly; error bars/CIs assume approximate normality.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Gives the hypothesis-testing counterpart to rliable's CI-first stance: when a p-value is required (e.g. paper tables comparing BC vs DT vs DAgger vs PPO), use Welch's t-test on per-run aggregates with α=0.01 and Bonferroni/Holm correction, with ≥20 training seeds for 1-SD effects; never run the bootstrap 'test' on <20 runs even though bootstrap CIs are fine for description.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.06979

### 2.3 Colas et al. ('How Many Random Seeds?', 2018) show with a worked Half-Cheetah example that at N=5 seeds the probability of missing a real effect (type-II error) was β=0.51, falling to 0.19 only at N=10; their final recommendations: prefer Welch's t-test over the bootstrap CI test, use α<0.05, correct for multiple comparisons, 'use at least n=20 samples in the pilot study' to estimate variances, and choose N larger than the power analysis prescribes.

They note all deep-RL papers reviewed by Henderson et al. 'used 5 seeds or less' and that 'the bootstrap test should not be used with less than 20 samples'. Power analysis procedure: estimate s1,s2 from a pilot, pick effect size ε and α, compute N for β=0.2; small pilots underestimate standard deviations, so inflate N.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Supports a seed budget rule for the papers: run a 20-seed pilot of the main comparison (cheap in Furrow) to estimate variance, then size the final experiment via power analysis, and report that computation in the appendix.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08295

### 2.4 Henderson et al. ('Deep RL that Matters', AAAI 2018) ran TRPO on HalfCheetah-v1 with 10 seeds, split them into two groups of 5 and obtained 'statistically different distributions just from varying random seeds' (2-sample t-test t=−9.0916, p=0.0016); their survey table shows published trial counts of 3, 5, '3-9', 'top-5' and 'top-2, top-3'.

They warn that reporting the maximum return or averaging over N<5 trials is 'potentially misleading', recommend bootstrap confidence intervals (10k bootstrap iterations) and bootstrap power analysis to decide whether more trials are needed, Welch's t-test / bootstrap percent-difference CIs for significance, and reporting all hyperparameters and implementation details; they explicitly do not recommend the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (per Colas et al.) and distinguish the 'online view' (average over training) from the 'policy optimization view' (offline evaluation of the final policy).

- confidence: high
- relevance: Canonical citation for why both papers must use ≥10 training seeds, never select top-k runs, and separate training-time performance from held-out evaluation of the final policy.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560

### 2.5 Patterson, Neumann, White & White ('Empirical Design in RL', JMLR 2024) conclude: 'in almost all cases 5 runs is insufficient to make strong claims, but even 30 runs can be insufficient if the distribution is heavily skewed'; 'we advocate that you do not report standard errors' (they are 'a low-confidence confidence interval'); in their DQN/PuddleWorld example ~20 runs were needed for accurate bootstrap CIs and 30 for an accurate mean, while a Bernstein CI needed 1000 runs vs 30 for percentile bootstrap.

Further guidance: use paired tests when methods share seeds/start states ('pairing allows us to account for sources of variation due to the environment or initialization' and is more powerful than checking CI overlap); apply Bonferroni (δ/5 for five baselines) for multiple comparisons or narrow the set of baselines; report tolerance intervals when per-run variability (not just the mean) matters; 'Consider reporting performance versus steps of interaction, rather than performance versus episodes'; 'Averaging over 3 or 5 runs: sometimes it is OK ... if the agent and environment are both deterministic. In the vast majority of cases this is a risky practice.' Also: tune all baselines, report hyperparameter sensitivity, and do not reuse other papers' numbers.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Because every method in our pipeline is evaluated on the same fixed held-out seed set, a paired design (per-seed differences, paired bootstrap CI / paired Welch) is the statistically correct and most powerful comparison; also motivates reporting tolerance intervals for days-to-complete across training seeds (speedrun reliability), not only the mean.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01315

### 2.6 Gorsane et al. (NeurIPS 2022) propose a concrete standardised protocol: R=10 independent training runs ('from Agarwal et al.'), E=32 evaluation episodes at every evaluation interval (every 10,000 timesteps), 'absolute performance' from 10×E=320 episodes of the best policy, min-max normalisation to [0,1] across algorithms, an (R × |tasks|) evaluation matrix per algorithm, then IQM and optimality gap with 95% stratified bootstrap CIs, probability of improvement and performance profiles via rliable, plus sample-efficiency curves.

Reporting requirements: 'All hyperparameters, code-level optimisations, computational requirements and framework details'; tables of normalised absolute performance per task with 95% CI and IQM per environment; public raw evaluation data and code; 'all baselines must be tuned fairly with the same compute budget'; results 'should be interpreted as a function of the compute budget used' (citing Dodge et al.). Their 75-paper meta-analysis found roughly 71% of papers report the number of independent runs but only ~27% report the evaluation interval/frequency, and they recommend preferring environments that test generalisation (Procgen-style) or else fixed, designer-specified task sets.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Provides defensible default numbers to copy into svrl.pipeline/evaluate.py (10 training seeds, periodic evaluation on a fixed seed set, a larger final evaluation, an (runs × tasks) matrix to rliable) and a reporting template; since Furrow is ~10^3× faster than SMAC we can exceed these defaults (e.g. 1,000 held-out seeds per final evaluation).
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.10485

### 2.7 Procgen (Cobbe et al., ICML 2020) defines the generalisation protocol: 'we train on a finite set of levels and we test on the full distribution of levels' — 500 training levels (hard) / 200 (easy), 200M timesteps (hard) / 25M (easy, 'approximately 3 GPU-hrs'); 'agents strongly overfit to small training sets ... To close the generalization gap, agents need access to as many as 10,000 levels'; results are 'mean and standard deviation ... across 3 seeds' (4 seeds for the training-set-size sweep).

Aggregate score: per-game normalized return R_norm=(R−R_min)/(R_max−R_min) with constants chosen to bound R, then the mean normalized return across games ('provides a better signal than the median, and since there is no need to be robust to outliers'). At test time 'we simply remove the determinism in the level sequence, instead choosing level sequences at random'.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Template for Furrow: a fixed finite set of training seeds (and a sweep over its size: 100 … 10,000) versus evaluation on disjoint seeds from the full distribution, reported separately for RestorationMode::Fixed and Remixed; also the min-max normalisation idea for turning 'days' into a [0,1] score across tasks.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.01588

### 2.8 The NetHack Learning Environment paper requires held-out-seed evaluation: 'Since the environment is procedurally generated and stochastic, evaluating on held-out unseen seeds ensures we test systematic generalization of agents' and 'We ask to report the average score obtained on 1000 episodes of randomly sampled and previously unseen seeds', along with the full character specification, options, allowed/hard-coded actions and the number of training seeds; training on a limited seed set shows the train/test gap 'becomes narrow when training with at least 1000 seeds'.

NLE results are reported as 'mean return of the last 100 episodes averaged over five runs'; generalisation analysis trains on limited seed sets and tests on 100 held-out seeds. They declare NLE solved only when agents 'consecutively ascend (ten episodes without retry) ... on unseen seeds', i.e. a reliability criterion, and explicitly discuss that in-game score is only a proxy that expert humans can minimise while winning.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Supports (a) a 1,000-unseen-seed final evaluation per policy, (b) mandatory disclosure of which macro actions are scripted (our macro controller and expert) and how many training seeds/demonstrations were used, (c) a reliability criterion (e.g. 'completes the Mill in ≤ X days on 10 consecutive unseen seeds') alongside averages.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13760

### 2.9 Crafter (Hafner, ICLR 2022) scores agents by the geometric mean of per-achievement success rates within a fixed budget: S = exp((1/N) Σ_i ln(1+s_i)) − 1 with s_i∈[0,100] and N=22; success rates are 'the fraction of training episodes during which the agent has unlocked the achievement at least once ... across all episodes that lead up to the budget of 1M environment steps'; 'the score is computed for each seed separately before averaging over seeds'; baselines use 10 seeds, trained <24 h on one GPU; human experts score 50.5±6.8 (100 episodes from 5 experts, grouped into 5 pseudo-seeds) vs DreamerV2 10.0±1.2, PPO 4.6±0.3, Rainbow 4.3±0.2, Random 1.6±0.0.

Rationale: 'Unlocking difficult achievements, even if it happens rarely, should contribute more than increasing the success rate of achievements that are already unlocked frequently' ('collecting a diamond 1% of the time instead of 0% is a meaningful improvement, whereas collecting wood 95% of the time instead of 90% is not'). Caveat acknowledged: reward-maximising agents are judged by something they only indirectly optimise. Fixed budget justified because allowing more steps 'would result in a comparison of compute resources rather than algorithm quality'. Episodes end at a 10,000-step time limit.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Gives a principled partial-progress metric for policies that rarely finish (early BC, LLM agents in NeMo Gym): geometric mean over wings (or requests) of completion rates within max_days, computed per training seed then averaged; also a template for a human-expert baseline collected through the SDV bridge and for fixing an interaction budget in the Furrow benchmark paper.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.06780

### 2.10 The NeurIPS 2021 NetHack Challenge ranked submissions by a tuple 'in order of tie-breaking: number of ascensions, median in-game score, and mean in-game score', evaluated on 512 episodes within a 2-hour window in development and on 4,096 episodes over a day in the test phase; the winner's median was 5,300 ('only just above a Beginner score'), symbolic bots' median was 'nearly threefold that of the neural bot's', and organisers observed metric gaming ('early termination of episodes beyond a certain internal score so as not to run down the clock').

Performance was 'very heavy-tailed' and strongly role-dependent (1 in 20 of the winner's Valkyries scored >30,000). Organisers recommend future metrics with 'checkpointing of achievements' so rankings reflect actual game progress rather than score. Appendix shows box plots (median, IQR, 5-95th percentile whiskers) per role and per dungeon depth.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Lessons for our leaderboard: (1) rank by a lexicographic tuple that puts the true objective first (completion rate, then median days, then mean), (2) use thousands of evaluation episodes when the environment is cheap, (3) define the completion event and episode termination unambiguously so days cannot be gamed (e.g. by truncation/passing out), (4) show distributions (box/violin or KM curves), not only means, because days-to-complete will be heavy-tailed.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11889

### 2.11 MineRL 2019 (ObtainDiamond) scored each submission as 'the average reward achieved by that submission over 100 episodes', retrained every finalist from scratch for six days (Azure NC6/NC12) under 'a strict environment sample limit of 8 million frames (or roughly 110 hours of in-game interactions), as well as limiting computation to 144 hours of wall clock time', on a held-out environment with a perturbed action space and unseen texture pack; Round-2 scores had mean 25.84, SD 17.37, and 'no team obtained a diamond'. BASALT 2022 instead ranked agents by TrueSkill ratings fitted to pairwise human judgements (MTurk) of videos on fixed world seeds, normalised per task and summed.

MineRL used shaped milestone rewards (sub-items on the path to diamond), so the 'average reward' is effectively a weighted progress score. BASALT asked judges to justify choices and vetted them to filter low-quality answers, and recommends that setup for human evaluations.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Sets precedents for (a) fixing sample and wall-clock budgets as part of the benchmark definition (Furrow paper), (b) evaluating generalisation under perturbed conditions (Remixed request lists, re-seeded maps), (c) using human pairwise preference + TrueSkill if we ever need qualitative evaluation of real-game trajectories, and (d) reporting SD/range across evaluation episodes, not a bare mean.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05012, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.13512

### 2.12 VPT (Baker et al., 2022) reports time-boxed success rates for a speedrun-like tech tree and explicitly hits the censoring problem: 'The median time to obtain a diamond pickaxe is unknown (except that it is > 20m) because contractors obtained this item in less than 50% of 20-minute episodes'; its RL-finetuned agent 'achieves over 80% reliability on iron pickaxes, almost 20% reliability on collecting diamonds, and 2.5% reliability on obtaining a diamond pickaxe' within 10-minute episodes, versus humans at 57%, 15%, 12% in 20-minute episodes; error bars are '95% confidence intervals of the mean, over 1,000 episodes per conditioning variant'.

Each tech-tree item is reported as the % of episodes that obtain it within the time budget, with the human median time and % within 10 minutes per item (Fig. 6). DreamerV3 similarly fixes Minecraft episodes at 'up to 36000 steps equaling 30 minutes', reports 'the episode return measured at 100M env steps, corresponding to about 100 days of in-game time', uses '10 seeds for Minecraft to reliably report the fraction of runs that achieve diamonds' (5 seeds elsewhere), shows 'mean over seeds with one standard deviation shaded', and tabulates compute per benchmark in GPU-days on a single A100 (Minecraft 8.9, Atari 7.7, ProcGen 16.1).

- confidence: high
- relevance: Direct analogues for our objective: report P(wing k completed by day d) milestone curves and success-within-budget for several budgets, distinguish 'fraction of episodes' from 'fraction of training runs' that ever complete, use ≥10 seeds when the headline number is a rare-success fraction, and report GPU-days per method. VPT's unidentifiable median shows why naive 'mean days among finishers' is wrong when success <100% — motivating Kaplan-Meier/RMST below.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.11795, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04104

### 2.13 Anderson et al. (2018, 'On Evaluation of Embodied Navigation Agents') define SPL = (1/N) Σ_i S_i · ℓ_i / max(p_i, ℓ_i) (success weighted by normalised inverse path length, with ℓ_i the shortest-path length and p_i the agent's path) and 'recommend adopting SPL as the primary measure', noting it is 'a rather stringent measure' (0.5 is good on unseen environments) that 'can be calibrated more reliably on each dataset by measuring the SPL of human subjects'; they also require an explicit 'DONE' action so success is judged at the agent's declared end, not 'at some favorable preceding time'.

Worked examples: 50% success with optimal paths → SPL 0.5; 100% success at 2× optimal length → 0.5; 50% success at 2× length → 0.25. Recommended auxiliary measures: success rate as a function of normalised path length, distribution of ℓ_i/max(p_i,ℓ_i), and sweeps of the success threshold.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Translates one-to-one into a 'Success weighted by inverse Days' score for the restoration objective: SPD = (1/N) Σ S_i · d*_i / max(d_i, d*_i) with d*_i a per-seed reference (expert planner's or best-known days), which rewards finishing and finishing fast in one bounded number and is robust to failures; the 'DONE' rule maps to counting completion only at the explicit final Deliver event.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.06757

### 2.14 Dolan & Moré (2002) performance profiles — the construct rliable adapts — were designed for exactly 'time to solve with failures': for each problem p and solver s the ratio r_{p,s} = t_{p,s} / min_s t_{p,s} is computed, failures are assigned a large r_M, and ρ_s(τ) = fraction of problems with r_{p,s} ≤ τ is plotted; ρ_s(1) is the probability of being the fastest solver and the plateau ρ*_s is the probability of solving a problem at all.

'The choice of r_M does not affect the performance evaluation'; profiles are nondecreasing step functions, 'a plot of the performance profile reveals all of the major performance characteristics'.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Gives a one-figure summary tailored to speedrunning: per held-out seed compute each method's days ÷ best method's days on that seed, treat failures as r_M, and plot ρ(τ): the left intercept shows how often a method is the fastest, the plateau its completion rate, and the curve shape its tail behaviour — complementing rliable's score-distribution profiles.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0102001

### 2.15 Survival-analysis tooling handles 'days to complete' with censored (unfinished) episodes without bias: lifelines' KaplanMeierFitter takes an event_observed indicator for right-censoring, exposes median_survival_time_ and exponential-Greenwood confidence intervals; lifelines.statistics provides logrank_test (null: same hazard at every time), multivariate_logrank_test, pairwise_logrank_test and survival_difference_at_fixed_point_in_time_test; lifelines.utils.restricted_mean_survival_time computes the area under the survival curve up to a finite t 'because the tail of a survival function has high variance'.

Bland & Altman (BMJ 2004) note the log-rank test 'cannot provide an estimate of the size of the difference between the groups or a confidence interval' and that effect sizes come from the hazard ratio/Cox model. Royston & Parmar (2013) show RMST (area under the survival curve to t*) is 'valid under any distribution of the time to event', the between-group RMST difference equals the area between curves and is read in time units, the hazard ratio is misleading when hazards are non-proportional, and 'the primary estimate of the RMST is specifically aligned to a chosen t* and this must be made explicit' to prevent picking t* for the most significant result.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Core of the recommended metric: treat each evaluation episode as (T_i = day of completion, δ_i = completed) censored at max_days; report the Kaplan-Meier completion curve 1−S(d), the median days (identifiable whenever success >50%), and the restricted mean days-to-completion at t*=max_days (= mean of min(T_i, max_days), the natural 'failures count as max_days' statistic) with CIs; compare methods with the log-rank test plus RMST-difference effect size; pre-register t*.
- sources: https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Survival%20analysis%20with%20lifelines.html, https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/lifelines.statistics.html, https://lifelines.readthedocs.io/en/latest/lifelines.utils.html, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC403858/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3922847/

### 2.16 The Pokémon Red RL paper (Pleines et al., 2025) — the closest published 'long-horizon game milestones' evaluation — repeats each experiment 5 seeds, evaluates '25 time points using 30 episodes per repetition', plots 'mean completion curves for distinct milestones' versus training steps, and tabulates per-milestone completion rates alongside 'steps to milestone' conditioned on success (e.g. humans 11,188±5,224 steps to Cerulean City vs the Fast agent 18,523±4,280), while admitting that 'if Figure 3 rendered standard deviations, the performances across different run types would be difficult to distinguish'.

30 human playthroughs serve as the baseline row (7/7 milestones). Their 'steps to milestone' numbers are means over runs that reached the milestone, which is exactly the success-conditioned statistic that becomes biased when completion rates differ between methods.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Illustrates both the right instinct (milestone completion curves, human baseline row) and the pitfall to avoid (reporting time-to-milestone only among finishers, 5 seeds, no CIs); our tables should pair each wing's completion rate with a censoring-aware time statistic (KM median or restricted mean) rather than a finishers-only mean.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19920

### 2.17 Conditioning calibration should be evaluated explicitly: the Decision Transformer paper plots achieved return against the requested target return (Fig. 4) and reports that 'the desired target returns and the true observed returns are highly correlated' and on some tasks 'almost perfectly match'; METR's time-horizon methodology fits a logistic model p_success = σ((log h_agent − log t_task)·β) to success-vs-task-length data and reports the budget at which success is 50% (and 80%).

BALROG (ICLR 2025) standardises LLM-agent game results as a 0-100 'progress' per task averaged across tasks with standard errors from 5-20 replicate seeds, and replaces NetHack's raw score with a data-informed progression metric (probability that a human who reached a given dungeon/experience level went on to win).

- confidence: high
- relevance: For the time-conditioned transformer: plot achieved days vs requested days_to_go (identity line), and fit P(complete within d days | requested d) to report the 'd50/d80 budgets' — a scalar 'speedrun horizon' comparable across methods; for NeMo Gym LLM agents: report 0-100 progress (e.g. weighted slots/requests/wings) with standard errors over replicate seeds, mirroring BALROG.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01345, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14499, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.13543

### 2.18 Compute and tuning-budget reporting has explicit standards: the NeurIPS paper checklist asks for 'the type of compute workers CPU or GPU, internal cluster, or cloud provider', 'the amount of compute required for each of the individual experimental runs', whether 'the full research project required more compute than the experiments reported', and for error bars to state the factors of variability, the method, and whether they are std-dev or SEM (preferring '2-sigma error bars' to '96% CI' phrasing); Green AI (Schwartz et al.) asks papers to 'plot accuracy as a function of computational cost and of training set size' and report the 'computational price tag'; Dodge et al. ('Show Your Work') recommend reporting expected validation performance as a function of the number of hyperparameter trials and provide a reporting checklist (none of 50 sampled EMNLP 2018 papers met all items).

Dodge et al. argue a comparison is only conclusive 'if two models have similar budgets' or the smaller-budget model wins; Gorsane et al. and Patterson et al. both require tuning all baselines with equal budgets. Procgen, Crafter and DreamerV3 report GPU-hours/GPU-days per run as part of the benchmark description.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Our pipeline already logs wall-clock, commit and content hash per stage; the papers should surface per-method GPU-hours, env steps, demonstrations consumed, Furrow steps/s, total project compute (incl. failed runs), and hyperparameter-search budgets, and present at least one 'performance vs compute/data budget' plot (e.g. success@192 vs number of demonstration episodes and vs PPO env steps).
- sources: https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.10597, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.03004, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.10485

### 2.19 For small real-game sample sizes, binomial success rates need proper interval methods: statsmodels' proportion_confint offers normal (the default), agresti_coull, beta (Clopper-Pearson: 'has coverage at least 1-alpha, but is in general conservative'), wilson ('contained in [0, 1] mathematically'), jeffreys and binom_test; 'most of the other methods have average coverage equal to 1-alpha, but will have smaller coverage in some cases'.

The default 'normal' (Wald) interval is the one to avoid at small n and extreme rates; Wilson or Jeffreys are the standard small-n choices (recommendation ours; the statsmodels page itself does not rank methods).

- confidence: medium
- relevance: SDV-bridge evaluation is real-time-bound, so real-game success rates will come from tens of episodes: report Wilson (or Jeffreys) 95% intervals and a KM curve rather than a bare percentage; the same applies to per-wing completion rates for weak policies with near-0% or near-100% success.
- sources: https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_confint.html

**Recommendations.**

- Define the primary outcome per evaluation episode as a censored time-to-event: T_i = in-game day on which the last wing is completed (counted only at the explicit final Deliver event, analogous to SPL's 'DONE' rule), δ_i = 1 if completed before max_days (192) else censored at 192 (truncation counts as failure, passing out does not end the episode). Pre-register t* = 192 days (Royston & Parmar) and never pick the horizon after seeing results. Also log per-wing completion days for milestone curves.
- Evaluation design: a fixed, disjoint held-out seed set (e.g. seeds ≥ 2^40, 1,000 seeds for Fixed and 1,000 for Remixed; NLE uses 1,000 unseen-seed episodes, NetHack Challenge 4,096), identical for every method so comparisons are paired; a separate validation seed set for checkpoint/hyperparameter selection (never the test seeds); training-seed-set size sweeps (100, 1k, 10k seeds, Procgen-style) to measure the generalisation gap. Each method is trained with R ≥ 10 independent training seeds (rliable/Gorsane default; use 20-30 for the headline BC vs DT vs DAgger vs PPO comparison since Furrow is cheap, and run a 20-seed pilot for power analysis per Colas et al.).
- Primary metrics (all with 95% intervals): (1) completion rate at 192 days, Wilson CI per run and stratified-bootstrap CI across runs; (2) Kaplan-Meier completion curve 1−S(d) with exponential-Greenwood bands; (3) restricted mean days-to-completion RMDC(192) = mean of min(T_i,192) (area under S up to t*), which charges failures 192 days; (4) KM median days (report 'undefined (>192)' when completion <50%, as VPT did); (5) Success-weighted inverse Days SPD = mean_i S_i · d*_i/max(d_i, d*_i) with d*_i = expert planner's days on seed i (or best known), calibrated against the expert and human runs; (6) rliable IQM + optimality gap of a normalised speed score (e.g. (192 − min(T,192))/(192 − d*)) over the (training runs × seed-strata) matrix; (7) for weak policies and LLM agents, a Crafter-style geometric mean of wing completion rates computed per seed then averaged.
- Statistical comparison protocol: lead with interval estimates and effect sizes, not p-values (Agarwal et al.). Report rliable's average probability of improvement P(X>Y) with CIs and percentile-bootstrap CIs on paired per-seed differences (ΔIQM, ΔRMDC, ΔSPD). For time-to-completion, use the log-rank test (lifelines logrank_test; pairwise_logrank_test with Holm/Bonferroni across methods) and report the RMST difference with CI as the effect size (hazard ratios only if proportional hazards is checked). When a scalar test is wanted on per-run aggregates, use Welch's t-test at α = 0.01 (or a paired t-test given shared seeds) with ≥ 20 training seeds for ~1-SD effects, and never the bootstrap 'test' below N=20-50 runs or the permutation test below N=10 (Colas et al.). Correct all multi-baseline comparisons (Bonferroni/Holm).
- Figures: (a) KM completion curves per method (Fixed and Remixed panels), with expert, random and human reference curves; (b) per-wing milestone completion curves vs day (VPT/Pokémon style); (c) Dolan-Moré performance profile of days ÷ best-method days per seed with failures at r_M; (d) rliable score-distribution profiles and the IQM/optimality-gap/probability-of-improvement interval plot; (e) sample-efficiency curves versus environment steps (not episodes) for PPO/DAgger and versus number of demonstration episodes for BC/DT, with IQM and stratified-bootstrap bands; (f) time-conditioning calibration: achieved days vs requested days_to_go with identity line, plus the logistic fit P(complete within d | request d) and the derived d50/d80 budgets; (g) tolerance intervals or per-run strip plots of RMDC across training seeds to show speedrun reliability; (h) performance vs compute and vs data budget plots (Green AI/Dodge).
- Tables: one row per method × mode with completion@192 [CI], KM median days [CI], RMDC(192) [CI], SPD [CI], IQM speed score [CI], geometric-mean wing progress, env steps, demos used, training GPU-hours, evaluation episodes, training seeds; rank rows lexicographically (completion rate, then median days, then mean) as the NetHack Challenge did. Add human (SDV recordings), scripted expert (noise 0) and random baselines, and a separate real-game table with Wilson CIs for the small SDV-bridge sample.
- Reporting hygiene (NeurIPS checklist, Gorsane, Patterson, Dodge): state factors of variability for every error bar (training seeds vs evaluation seeds) and the CI method; never report standard errors; report hardware, GPU-hours per run and in total (including failed runs), Furrow throughput, wall-clock per stage, hyperparameter-search budgets for every baseline (equal budgets), content hash/env version, which macro actions are scripted, and the number of training seeds/demonstrations; publish raw per-episode (seed, T_i, δ_i, per-wing days) data and the analysis notebook; numbers in the Typst papers come only from results.json.
- Implementation in svrl.evaluate/report: write per-episode records (method, train_seed, eval_seed, mode, days, success, wing_days[], ticks, return, wall-clock); compute KM/log-rank/RMST with lifelines, binomial CIs with statsmodels (wilson), and IQM/profiles/probability-of-improvement with rliable on a (train_seeds × eval_seed_strata) matrix (reps=50000); fix the evaluation protocol in docs/ENV.md so the Furrow paper's leaderboard (budget in env steps, demo count, 1,000 held-out seeds, Fixed vs Remixed) cannot drift between papers (rliable Fig. 5 shows protocol changes alone can explain reported gains).

## Angle 3: Across the closest analogues to "speedrun a long-horizon collection objective from demonstrations" (NetHack, Minecraft diamond, Crafter/Craftax), the evidence i

**Summary.** Across the closest analogues to "speedrun a long-horizon collection objective from demonstrations" (NetHack, Minecraft diamond, Crafter/Craftax), the evidence is consistent: (1) plain behaviour cloning from even billions of expert transitions recovers only a fraction of the demonstrator (NetHack BC 554-1,318 vs AutoAscend 8.5k-10k; CrafterDojo BC ~61% score vs 97.5% expert on 180M expert steps), and BC performance scales as a shallow power law that is forecast to plateau below the expert; (2) offline RL (CQL/IQL/AWAC/REM) is worse than BC on expert-like game data in every NetHack study, and flat offline RL fails to scale with data on long horizons unless the effective horizon is reduced; (3) the two interventions with the largest documented gains are on-policy expert correction (DAgger with a privileged expert: 20% -> 96-100% in Learning-by-Cheating; +50 pp in Roach) and RL fine-tuning anchored by a KL/kickstart term to the imitation prior (VPT: diamond pickaxe 2.5% only with KL, collapse without; NetHack APPO+BC 2-3x over BC); (4) return/time-conditioned imitation (Decision Transformer / RvS) is provably fragile under stochastic dynamics and needs return coverage, which matters for a days_to_go-conditioned speedrun policy in an environment with random weather and merchant stock; (5) hierarchy/macro-actions and horizon reduction help (hierarchy+RL best on NetHack; all top MineRL teams used action reduction and most used hierarchy); (6) model-free PPO-RNN with ~1B steps and DreamerV3 with ~100M steps are the strongest no-demonstration controls (Craftax-Classic ~90% of optimal reward in <1 GPU-hour; DreamerV3 diamonds in 100M steps on 1 A100 in 9 days) and are affordable at Furrow's throughput; (7) LLM agents are near zero on NetHack zero-shot (~1.5%) and modest on Crafter (~33%), and RL-training them (GRPO) is unstable at long horizons unless the horizon is curriculum-scheduled or step-level credit/turn-level PPO is used; NeMo Gym's resources-server protocol (/seed_session, tools, /verify) and NeMo RL GRPO fit the DESIGN.md plan. A convincing result for this project is a learned policy that exceeds the scripted expert's success rate (>=95%) and median days on held-out seeds, approaches a search-derived lower bound on days, shows calibrated time-conditioning, and transfers to the real game through the SDV bridge, with all GPU-hours reported.

### 3.1 Behaviour cloning on NetHack from billions of bot transitions recovers only ~5-10% of the demonstrator's score, and offline RL (CQL/IQL/offline DQN) is far worse than BC; adding online RL on top of BC roughly doubles BC but stays far below the expert.

Dungeons and Data (NeurIPS 2022 D&B): NLD-AA = 109,545 AutoAscend games / ~3.48B transitions (96.7 GB compressed); NLD-NAO = 1,511,228 human games / ~9.86B transitions (no action labels). AutoAscend mean 10,105 / median 5,422. BC on NLD-AA: 554±142 (random role), 1,058±503 (Human Monk, dataset average 17,274). CQL 33/56, IQL 160/201, offline DQN 0.0. Online APPO 868/2,046; APPO+BC 1,282/2,809; Kickstarting+BC 962/2,090. Authors attribute offline-RL failure to stochasticity, partial observability, procedural generation and the huge state/action space; BC does better because its supervised signal is denser and less noisy.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Sets expectations for Furrow stage 3 (train_bc): even with 10^9 expert macro-steps, pure BC should be expected to under-recover the Rust expert; offline RL (IQL/CQL) should be a low-priority control, not a headline method.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2211.00539v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00539

### 3.2 Scaling BC on NetHack follows clean power laws in model size, data and compute, but the return curve is forecast to plateau well below the expert; the best scaled transformer BC reaches ~7.8k (Human Monk) vs an expert score of ~17k.

Tuyls et al. (v3): NLD-AA extended to ~55B samples; transformer BC models from 200k to 200M parameters on H100 80GB; loss/return power laws with exponents alpha~0.6 (params), beta~0.4 (samples). Reported offline Human Monk table: Scaled-BC 7,784 vs prior best diff-History LM 4,504, Transformer BC 1,974, CDGPT5 BC 1,058, IQL 201, CQL 56. Forecast @40B/@55B-sample models took ~5 and ~6.5 days. Authors state the return power law 'will plateau well before expert performance in NetHack (but not in Atari)'; context lengths up to 4096 and inventory features helped. Note: the diff-History paper (v3) quotes Tuyls' earlier XL-LSTM at 2,740 on 115B actions, so cross-paper numbers differ by version/setting.

- confidence: medium
- relevance: Plan to measure a BC scaling curve (success and days vs. number of demos and parameters) rather than a single BC number; the plateau argues for DAgger/RL on top of BC rather than only more demos.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2307.09423v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09423, https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07540v3

### 3.3 On NetHack, history (Transformer-LSTM) doubles flat BC, hierarchical strategy labels help the LSTM, and RL fine-tuning from BC gives the best neural result (1,551, ~18% of the symbolic expert); scaling model size alone did not help and even hurt.

NetHack is Hard to Hack (NeurIPS 2023), HiHack dataset ~109,907 games / ~3.24B transitions with 13 strategy labels from AutoAscend. AutoAscend mean 8,556±187 (median 4,918). Flat BC: LSTM 658±41, Transformer-LSTM 1,318±38. Hierarchical BC: LSTM 931±42, Transformer-LSTM 1,151±43 (hierarchy hurt the transformer). APPO fine-tuning: LSTM 1,204±138, hierarchical LSTM 1,551±73 (best), Transformer-LSTM 1,326±28 (no significant gain, p~0.42). A 6-layer 98.9M model scored worse than a 3-layer 50.8M model; data scaling sub-log-linear, plateau ~1,000. Diagnosed causes: lack of hierarchical control, weak multimodal representations, no error correction; multi-modal demonstrations confound BC.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Supports the SpeedrunTransformer-with-history design, and suggests logging the expert's active priority rule (which of its 8 priorities fired) as a cheap strategy label for hierarchical/auxiliary BC; expect RL fine-tuning to help most where BC underfits.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2305.19240

### 3.4 A dedicated offline-RL benchmark on NetHack (Katakomba) found no offline RL algorithm meaningfully better than behaviour cloning; recurrent CQL, IQL, AWAC and REM all stayed far from the expert and the best CQL settings were the ones closest to BC.

Katakomba (NeurIPS 2023 D&B): 38 role/race/alignment tasks repacked from the AutoAscend dataset (~680 episodes, ~1.3 GB per task). Recurrent BC, IQL, CQL, REM, AWAC; 3 seeds, 50 eval episodes, 500k gradient steps (~20 epochs). Normalized scores below 6.0 on average; REM never non-zero; ~5% of episodes reach normalized ~20. Conclusion quoted: 'there is not much improvement beyond naive behavioral cloning'. Compute: 64 nodes each 1x A100 / 14 CPUs / 128 GB RAM; in-RAM loading needs 51-129 GB.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Justifies treating IQL/CQL as a sanity control in Furrow and reporting with RLiable-style performance profiles rather than mean return.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2306.08772v2, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08772

### 3.5 In the NeurIPS 2021 NetHack Challenge (483 entrants, 631 submissions) symbolic bots took the top three places and the best symbolic median was ~3x the best neural median; even the winner (~5,300 median) was below the 12,200 needed to ascend.

Organizers' explanation: symbolic bots can encode 'strategy'-style subroutines with domain knowledge; neural agents struggle to discover strategy-like behaviour with a large action space and sparse reward; hierarchical RL and long-term credit assignment remain open. The winning neural-track entry (RAPH) was a hybrid: rule-based skills for eating/exploration/inventory plus a TorchBeast RL agent for fighting. Kakao Brain trained IMPALA policies from scratch for 10B steps per role. Sample Factory baseline ~700 mean vs TorchBeast ~400.

- confidence: high
- relevance: A hand-written macro-level expert (furrow-expert) is the realistic upper bound to imitate; the project's macro-action design mirrors what let hybrid agents win.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.11889, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11889

### 3.6 Representation and context design can buy orders of magnitude in data efficiency: a 127M-parameter GPT-2 fine-tuned on 64M AutoAscend actions with 'diff history' text observations matched a vision LSTM trained on 115B actions (1800x fewer samples).

diff History (Piterbarg et al.): LangHack = 10,000 AutoAscend games subsampled to 500K chunks of H=64 (LangHack-2x: 1M chunks, 8.46B tokens). Best agent mean 2,766±481 (median 1,802) vs Tuyls et al. XL-LSTM 2,740 on 115B actions; prior Transformer-LSTM 1,318 and CDGPT5 658 on 3.2B actions. Context extended to 4096 tokens; fine-tuning on 2x A100 within a 48-hour budget per run; doubling data gave +47% mean score, so it remains data-limited. Limitations: assumes text observations, no explicit planning.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Supports the compact deterministic Env::describe() text view and a 'diff' of state changes for the NeMo Gym LLM agent, and suggests small LMs fine-tuned on expert text traces as a cheap imitation baseline.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07540v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07540

### 3.7 VPT (IDM-labelled video -> BC foundation model -> RL fine-tune with KL to the frozen BC prior) reached a diamond pickaxe in 2.5% of 10-minute Minecraft episodes; without the KL term RL stalled after ~100k episodes and catastrophically forgot skills.

Baker et al. 2022: IDM trained on 1,962 contractor hours (~$20/h; ~4,500 h / ~$90k total contractor data); ~70k hours of filtered web video labelled; 0.5B-parameter BC model trained ~9 days on 720 V100s. RL fine-tune (248M-parameter policy): 80 GPUs + 56,719 CPUs for ~6 days, ~1.4M episodes of 12,000 frames (~16.8B frames). Results: iron pickaxe >80%, diamonds ~20%, diamond pickaxe 2.5% (humans 57%/15%/12%). BC-only zero-shot: 0.19 crafting tables per 60-min episode vs 5.44 for contractors. KL loss rho*KL(pi_pretrained || pi_theta) replaces the entropy bonus; rho starts at 0.2 and decays by 0.9995 per iteration; high rho blocks reward optimisation, low rho fails to prevent forgetting. Reward shaping: tiered item rewards (wood/stone 1, coal 2, iron 4, diamond 8) divided by capped quantities; deceptive-reward and sparse-reward failure modes documented; each config run once.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Direct template for Furrow stage 6 (PPO fine-tune with KL to the imitation prior): copy the KL-as-entropy-replacement trick with an annealed coefficient, and the per-slot/per-request/per-wing tiered reward in RewardConfig mirrors VPT's capped tiered shaping.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.11795

### 3.8 DreamerV3 collected Minecraft diamonds from scratch (no human data) within 100M environment steps using one A100 for ~9 days, where earlier RL baselines never passed the iron pickaxe.

Hafner et al. Contrast given in the paper: VPT needed human data and 720 GPUs for 9 days. Scaling experiments on Crafter vary model size 12M-400M and replay ratio. - confidence: high
- relevance: DreamerV3 (or a JAX world model) is the right no-demonstration model-based control for Furrow; at 20k macro steps/s, 100M steps of environment time is minutes, so the GPU learner is the bottleneck.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2301.04104v2, https://github.com/danijar/crafter

### 3.9 On Crafter-like benchmarks, from-scratch PPO-RNN with ~1B steps solves most of Craftax-Classic (~90% of optimal reward in under an hour on one GPU) but only ~15% of full Craftax even at 10B steps; a 2025 transformer world model is the first to exceed human-expert reward on Craftax-Classic at 1M steps.

Craftax (ICML 2024): Craftax-Classic ~405k steps/s and Craftax ~267k steps/s at 4096 workers (vs Crafter 1,580, NetHack 5,628, MineRL 133). Craftax-1B (% of max reward 226): PPO 11.9, PPO-RNN 15.3, ICM 11.9, E3B 11.0, RND 12.0; 10B steps 'barely affects performance' and the agent never enters the gnomish mines; exploration bonuses did not help (E3B hurt). Key tricks: Dyna with warmup, patch nearest-neighbour tokenizer, block teacher forcing; 8x H100 for main experiments; MFRL 11M steps in ~15 min on one A100. Crafter 1M-step leaderboard: human 50.5, SPRING (GPT-4) 27.3, Achievement Distillation 21.8, Curious Replay 19.4, PPO-ResNet 15.6, DreamerV3 14.5, PPO 4.6, random 1.6.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow's throughput targets (2M primitive / 20k macro steps/s) put it in the Craftax regime: PPO-RNN at 1B primitive steps or 100M macro steps is a cheap, mandatory from-scratch baseline, and the Craftax result that 10x more steps did not help predicts that pure RL will stall on multi-season, time-gated requests.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16801, https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16801, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MichaelTMatthews/Craftax/main/README.md, https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01591v3, https://github.com/danijar/crafter

### 3.10 CrafterDojo (2025) ran almost exactly the Furrow recipe (RL/scripted expert -> synthetic demos -> BC 'VPT' prior): BC on 20,000 expert episodes (~180M steps, ground-truth actions) recovered ~61% Crafter score vs the expert's 97.5%, for ~8 GPU-hours on 4x RTX 4090.

CrafterPlay: 20,000 episodes, ~180M timesteps, generated by a PPO-RNN expert trained for 10B steps in Craftax-Classic-Symbolic (~10 GPU-hours, one RTX 4090; expert score 97.5, return 98.4). CrafterVPT skips the IDM (labels are known) and does NLL behaviour cloning. Models: tiny 3.2M, base 15.9M, large 64.3M params. Scores: tiny 52.9±4.0 / return 66.8; base 61.0±3.0 / 71.8; large 61.4±4.7 / 71.3; human 50.5 / 65.0; Dedieu et al. 31.8 / 69.7; Delta-IRIS 9.3 / 35.0. Compute: tiny/base 4x RTX 4090 for 7-8.5 h; large 8x A6000 for 25 h. PPO-RNN from scratch shown near-zero on four long-horizon tasks; C-Steve-1 (goal-conditioned) trained ~1 h on 8x 4090.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Closest published quantitative template for Furrow stage 1+3: expect BC to land at roughly half to two-thirds of the expert's success on comparable data, and model size beyond ~16M params gave nothing; the gap is the target for DAgger and KL-RL.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2508.13530, https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13530

### 3.11 Return- or horizon-conditioned imitation (Decision Transformer / RvS) is only guaranteed to work under near-deterministic dynamics plus return coverage, with a suboptimality bound of epsilon*(1/alpha_f + 2)*H^2 and coverage that can be exponential in the horizon; in stochastic environments it 'fails dramatically' by imitating lucky trajectories.

Brandfonbrener et al. (NeurIPS 2022): RCSL needs transitions/rewards deterministic up to epsilon, return coverage alpha_f at the conditioning value, and consistency of the conditioning function; reaching a high conditioning value may need ~10^(H/2) samples; it cannot stitch trajectories (fails on 'stitch-hard' and antmaze), and TD3+BC/IQL generally beat BC/RCSL on D4RL except on small high-quality human datasets (pen-human) where DT wins. Paster et al. (ESPER, NeurIPS 2022): DT/RvS fail in stochastic environments because the conditioned return was achieved by luck; conditioning on learned cluster-average returns restores target-vs-achieved alignment on 2048 and Connect Four vs a stochastic opponent. Decision Transformer itself (Chen et al. 2021) conditions a causal transformer on return-to-go and matches/exceeds model-free offline RL on Atari/Gym/Key-to-Door.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow's days_to_go conditioning is exactly RCSL in a stochastic environment (weather, merchant stock, fishing draws): plan an alignment plot (requested vs achieved days), expect miscalibration at aggressive targets, and consider ESPER-style conditioning on expected days or conditioning on the expert 'style' instead of raw days.
- sources: https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.01079, https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01079, https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967, https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01345

### 3.12 DAgger-style on-policy correction with a privileged (full-state) expert is the single largest documented gain over offline BC in long-horizon control: BC cost grows as T^2*epsilon versus O(T*epsilon) for DAgger, and in CARLA it lifted success from 20% (direct imitation) to 96-100%.

Ross, Gordon & Bagnell 2011: Theorem 2.1 gives J(pi) <= J(pi*) + T^2*epsilon for supervised BC (tight); DAgger with N=O~(uT) iterations achieves uT*epsilon_N + O(1); the parameter-free schedule beta_i = I(i=1) often works best; Super Tux Kart fall-free after 15 iterations where supervised did not improve with data. Learning by Cheating (Chen et al. 2019): privileged BEV agent -> vision agent; ablation on CARLA navigation (test town+weather): direct imitation 20%, two-stage 16%, + white-box all-branch supervision 64%, + on-policy rollouts 96%, both 100%; NoCrash dense 85% vs CILRS 39%. Roach (Zhang et al. 2021): RL coach as expert with DAgger (5 iterations), soft action-distribution KL targets plus value and latent-feature supervision; NoCrash-dense new town/new weather 78±0 vs 28±4 for the Autopilot-supervised agent; a stronger expert raises the imitation ceiling.

- confidence: high
- relevance: The Rust expert is state-feedback and queryable on learner states (DESIGN.md §4), i.e., the LBC/Roach setting; DAgger (stage 5) should be the primary imitation method, using the expert's action distribution over styles as soft targets.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/1011.0686v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.12294, https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294, https://arxiv.org/html/2108.08265v3, https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.08265

### 3.13 Offline RL beats BC only under specific conditions (noisy/suboptimal data, few critical states, shifted initial states); with clean expert data no learner can beat BC's rate, and naive untuned CQL is usually worse than BC. Moreover flat offline RL does not scale with 1000x more data on long-horizon tasks unless the effective horizon is reduced.

Kumar et al. 2022: with expert data (C*=1) any learner suffers ~|S|H/N (Theorem 4.3); offline RL can win when critical states occur ~1/H of the time or data is noisy. Table 1 (expert data): manipulation close-open-grasp BC 17.4% vs naive CQL 20.0% vs tuned CQL 49.7%; AntMaze-medium diverse-init 55.2 vs 19.0 vs 67.0; AntMaze-large BC 4.83 vs CQL 0.0; Adroit door-human BC 86.3 vs tuned CQL 79.1. Noisy-expert CQL 85.7-92.4% vs BC-on-expert 14.5-33.2%. Park et al. 2025 (Horizon Reduction): OGBench cube/puzzle/humanoidmaze datasets up to ~1B transitions; flow BC, IQL, CRL, SAC+BC all plateau well below 100% and all fail cube-octuple even with ~591M-parameter models; combination-lock experiments show TD bias accumulating with horizon; SHARSA (n-step SARSA value + hierarchical subgoal policy from flow BC with rejection sampling) is the only method with non-trivial performance on all four tasks.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Run IQL/CQL only on the noisy-demo datasets (noise>0 styles) where theory predicts gains; the macro action space already shortens the horizon (~100s of macro steps per episode vs ~10^5 primitive ticks), which is the main lever that makes any value-based method viable here.
- sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2204.05618v1, https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05618, https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04168v1, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04168

### 3.14 Anchoring RL to a prior works in several forms with documented numbers: kickstarting gave ~10x data-efficiency and +42% final score on DMLab-30; RLPD (no pretraining, 50/50 offline/online sampling) gave up to 2.5x over prior offline-to-online methods and solved all AntMaze tasks in 300k steps while IQL+finetune stalled; AlphaStar's human-imitation policy already beat 84% of players before RL.

Schmitt et al. 2018: student matches from-scratch agent in ~10x fewer steps and exceeds its final performance by 42%, self-regulating so students surpass teachers. RLPD (Ball et al. 2023): symmetric 50/50 sampling, LayerNorm in critics, ensemble of 10, UTD 20, no offline pretraining and no constraint term; 2.5x on sparse Adroit Door; first to solve all D4RL AntMaze in <1/3 the step budget; IQL+finetune 'struggles to improve beyond' its initialization. - confidence: high
- relevance: Gives two concrete fine-tuning baselines for stage 6: (a) PPO with annealed KL to the frozen imitation policy (VPT/kickstart style) and (b) RLPD-style off-policy RL with the demo dataset in the replay buffer and no pretraining, as a check on whether pretraining is even needed.

### 3.16 When the simulator is deterministic and restorable, search-based exploration plus imitation/robustification yields far stronger demonstrations than any policy: Go-Explore reached 43,791 on Montezuma's Revenge without domain knowledge and 1,731,645 with it (above the 1.2M human world record), and resetting RL episodes to states along one demonstration (backward algorithm) reached 74,500.

Salimans & Chen 2018: start each episode from a demonstration state, moving the start backwards; reduces exploration cost from exponential to quadratic in the number of states between rewards; 74,500 high score. Expert Iteration (Anthony et al. 2017): tree search acts as the expert producing imitation targets for a policy/value net which then guides search; tabula-rasa agent beat MoHex 1.0 at Hex.

- confidence: high
- relevance: Furrow is bit-deterministic given (seed, actions) with <=50 us reset, so a beam/MCTS planner over macros with state cloning can produce per-seed near-optimal days-to-complete (a speedrun lower bound) and much faster demonstrations than the hand-written expert; backward-start curricula from those trajectories are a cheap way to make PPO fine-tuning work on the sparse completion reward.

**Recommendations.**

- Baseline suite, in order of implementation (easy -> hard): (0) random and scripted-expert (noise 0 and noisy/style variants) on held-out seeds; (1) a search planner over macros that clones Env state (beam search / MCTS, Go-Explore-style archive keyed on restoration progress) to get a per-seed days-to-complete lower bound and the fastest demonstrations; (2) MLP BC on expert macro demos with action masking; (3) SpeedrunTransformer BC with history (K~32-128 macro steps); (4) days_to_go-conditioned BC/DT; (5) DAgger with the Rust expert (beta_1 = 1 then beta = 0, 5-10 rounds, aggregate everything, soft targets = expert action distribution across styles); (6) PPO fine-tune from the DAgger policy with annealed KL to the frozen prior (start rho ~0.2, decay ~0.9995/iter, replace the entropy bonus as in VPT) plus an RLPD-style control (demo buffer at 50/50, no pretraining); (7) no-demo controls: PPO-RNN at 1B primitive / 100M macro steps and DreamerV3 at 100M steps; (8) offline RL (IQL, CQL) only on noisy-demo datasets as a control; (9) LLM agents: zero-shot on describe() text, then SFT on expert text traces, then GRPO via NeMo Gym with a horizon curriculum.
- Size the demonstration data for the BC scaling curve, not a single run: NetHack needed billions of transitions and still plateaued, and CrafterDojo's 180M expert steps gave ~61% of expert score; at the design target of 20k macro steps/s, 10^8-10^9 macro steps of expert demos is a few hours to a day of CPU, so generate a 10^6 / 10^7 / 10^8 / 10^9-step ladder and report success and median days vs. data (and vs. parameters at 3 sizes), expecting a shallow power law.
- Treat DAgger as the headline imitation method and make the expert's soft labels rich: log the expert's action distribution over styles and the active priority rule (harvest/deliver/time-gated/plant/machines/upgrade/money/sleep) at every learner-visited state; use the rule as an auxiliary 'strategy' head (HiHack-style hierarchy) and the distribution as a KL target (Roach-style). Expect the largest jump in the whole pipeline here (LBC: 20% -> 96-100%).
- Audit time-conditioning as RCSL: plot requested vs. achieved days on held-out seeds; expect degradation at aggressive targets because of weather/merchant stochasticity. Mitigations to ablate: condition on the expert style or a learned expected-days (ESPER-like cluster average) instead of raw days_to_go; add the cumulative days elapsed to the state (the consistency condition); check return coverage (how many demos exist near the requested days) before blaming the model.
- For PPO fine-tuning on a sparse completion objective, add a backward-start curriculum from expert/search trajectories (reset to late-episode states first, then earlier), which is cheap given deterministic replay, and keep the per-slot/per-request/per-wing shaped rewards capped so they cannot be farmed (VPT's capped tiered scheme). Always report a no-KL ablation to demonstrate catastrophic forgetting of early-game skills.
- Keep offline RL out of the critical path: run IQL/CQL once on clean expert data (expect worse than BC) and once on noisy/style-mixed data (where theory predicts it can win), using the macro action space (short horizon) and n-step targets; if it does not beat BC on either, say so and move on.
- Use Furrow's determinism as a scientific instrument: report the search planner's days as the per-seed speedrun bound and present every learned policy's days as a fraction of that bound; this also gives a principled way to grade 'speedrun' quality beyond 'faster than the hand-written expert'.
- For the NeMo Gym agent: implement the resources server as SimpleResourcesServer with /seed_session (seed from the task row), observe/list_actions/act tools and /verify (success + partial credit per slot + days penalty), or as a GymnasiumServer if per-turn rewards are needed; train a 3B-9B policy (Qwen2.5-3B/7B or Nemotron Nano 9B v2) on one 8x80GB node; SFT first on expert describe()-traces (diff-style text), then GRPO with a growing max-turn/max-day horizon, seed-diverse task rows, group size ~8, and monitoring of reward variance collapse; consider GiGPO-style step-level advantages or turn-level PPO if GRPO collapses.
- What would count as a convincing result: (a) on >=200 held-out Fixed-mode seeds, learned policy success >= expert's (>=95%) and median days strictly below the expert's median, with a CI; (b) days within ~10-20% of the search-planner bound on most seeds; (c) calibrated time-conditioning (achieved days track requested days over the feasible range); (d) DAgger and KL-RL each shown as a significant gain over BC on the scaling curve, with no-history, no-hierarchy and no-KL ablations; (e) learned policy beats PPO-RNN and DreamerV3 trained from scratch at matched environment steps; (f) generalisation to Remixed restoration mode and to the real game through the SDV bridge (success rate and in-game days on a fixed set of saves); (g) GPU-hours and CPU-hours per method reported alongside every number, as VPT/NLD/Katakomba do.
- Known failure modes to instrument explicitly in evaluate.py: compounding errors (success vs. episode length), deceptive shaping (reward farming of slots without completing wings), forgetting after RL (early-season skills), multimodal demos (styles) confusing BC (measure per-style policies vs mixed), and stochastic-conditioning miscalibration; log day-of-first-wing and days-between-wings so regressions are visible before final success is.
